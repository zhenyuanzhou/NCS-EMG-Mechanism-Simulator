# Source notes — Preston & Shapiro, Electromyography and Neuromuscular Disorders, 4th ed. (Elsevier 2020)
Page convention: "p.N" = printed page; PDF page = printed + 11 (verified on every body page).

## Ch 2 Anatomy & Neurophysiology
- p.16: Na channel inactivation within 1–2 ms → refractory period; unmyelinated CV 0.2–1.5 m/s.
- p.17: Saltatory conduction; internode ~1 mm, node 1–2 µm; myelinated human peripheral fibers 35–75 m/s.
- p.18: Motor unit = AHC + axon + all its muscle fibres; all fibres activated (all-or-none) → MUAP. NMJ: Ca influx → ACh release.
- p.19: Routine NCS measure the largest/fastest fibers; small fiber neuropathy may have normal NCS. Ia afferents only in mixed studies.
- p.20: All NCS/EMG potentials are extracellular recordings of intracellular events = volume conduction; near-field: amplitude increases closer to source; advancing AP → triphasic (+ − +) as it approaches, passes under, leaves the electrode; onset latency for nerve APs at first positive peak.
- p.21 (Fig 2.19, 2.20): depolarization beginning under G1 → biphasic initially negative (CMAP with G1 over motor point); G1 off motor point → initial positivity; source never passing under electrode → small positive deflection only. Convention: negative is UP. Far-field: stimulus artifact appears simultaneously at all sites.

## Ch 3 Basic NCS
- p.23: Motor gain 2–5 mV/div; belly-tendon montage, G1 over motor endplate, G2 over tendon; CMAP = summation of all muscle fiber APs; supramaximal +20%.
- p.24 (Fig 3.2): Latency = stimulus→initial deflection = (1) nerve conduction to NMJ + (2) NMJ delay + (3) muscle depolarization. Amplitude baseline→negative peak. Duration = onset→first baseline crossing (negative-peak duration). Negative-peak area. Latency/CV reflect fastest fibers; all fibers contribute to amplitude/area; duration = synchrony. Low CMAP also from conduction block, some NMJ disorders, myopathies.
- p.25 (Fig 3.3): CV = distance / (PL − DL); DL contains A+B+C, PL contains A+B+C+D.
- p.26: Sensory gain 10–20 µV/div; SNAP usually 1–50 µV; G1–G2 2.5–4 cm; onset latency to initial negative (biphasic) or initial positive peak (triphasic); sensory CV = distance/onset latency (single site).
- p.27 (Fig 3.7): CMAP negative-peak duration usually 5–6 ms; SNAP 1–2 ms (typ. ~1.5 ms). Low SNAP amplitude indicates a definite disorder of peripheral nerve.
- p.28–29 (Fig 3.10): Lesions proximal to DRG (root, cord, brain) → SNAP normal even with sensory loss; motor root lesion → motor fiber degeneration.
- p.30–32 (Fig 3.11, 3.12): Normal temporal dispersion & phase cancellation; proximal SNAPs much smaller/longer; single large sensory fiber negative duration ~0.5 ms vs distal SNAP ~1.3 ms; motor less affected (MUAP neg. duration 5–6 ms, narrower CV range). Dispersion alone would preserve area; phase cancellation reduces area too.
- p.32: supramaximal: plateau then +20–25%.
- p.33: Sensory or mixed NCS abnormalities always imply peripheral nerve disorder; motor abnormalities can be AHC, root, nerve, NMJ, muscle.
- p.34 (Fig 3.16–3.18): Axonal loss: amplitude ↓; CV normal or slightly ↓ but not <75% LLN; DL not >130% ULN. Median fastest ~65 m/s, slowest ~35 m/s. Reduced amplitude does not necessarily imply axonal loss. Fig 3.16 example normal DL<4.4 ms, amp>4 mV, CV>49 m/s (median, illustrative).
- p.34–35: Hyperacute axonal loss: NCS normal within 3–4 days if stim & record distal to lesion; Wallerian degeneration days 3–10; motor earlier (days 3–5) than sensory (days 6–10). First ~3 days: distal normal, proximal (across lesion) reduced = pseudo-conduction block; repeat after ~1 week.
- p.35: Demyelination: CV <75% LLN, DL >130% ULN; any CV <35 m/s arm / <30 m/s leg = unequivocal demyelination (except regenerating fibers after complete injury).
- p.36: Borderline slowing + very low amplitude → likely severe axonal loss. Sensory amplitudes low in demyelination via exaggerated dispersion/cancellation (marathon analogy).
- p.37 (Fig 3.20, 3.21): Block location vs stimulation sites (distal-to-distal-stim block mimics axonal; between sites → prox drop; proximal to all sites → normal CMAPs, late responses may be abnormal). Normal subjects: amplitude/area drop ≤20%, duration increase ≤15% (wrist–elbow, ankle–knee); Erb's point values doubled. Tibial popliteal: up to 50% amplitude drop in normals.
- p.38: Computer simulation (Rhee 1990): area can fall up to 50% from dispersion/cancellation alone → >50% area drop to define electrophysiologic conduction block. Conduction block & abnormal dispersion both signify acquired demyelination. Myopathy: sensory normal; CMAP generally normal (distal), low in distal/severe myopathy; DL/CV normal.
- p.39: Postsynaptic NMJ (MG): CMAP usually normal; presynaptic (LEMS, botulism): CMAP low at rest.

## Ch 4 Late responses
- p.41 (Table 4.1): F response: motor afferent & efferent; supramaximal stim; amplitude 1–5% of CMAP; variable latency/morphology; normal min latency ≤32 ms median/ulnar, ≤56 ms peroneal/tibial (assumes median height). H reflex: Ia sensory afferent, motor efferent; submaximal long-duration (1 ms) pulse; H present at low intensity without M; with higher intensity H decreases as M increases; tibial-soleus; ≤34 ms; side-to-side ≤1.5 ms; H/M ≤50%.
- p.42: F response occurs 25–32 ms (median/ulnar wrist), 45–56 ms (peroneal/tibial ankle); proximal stimulation → CMAP latency ↑, F latency ↓. F = antidromic to AHC, backfiring of small population of AHCs, orthodromic return; 1–5% of muscle fibers; no synapse — not a true reflex.
- p.43: F persistence normal 80–100%, always >50% (except peroneal); chronodispersion up to 4 ms arms / 6 ms legs; F may be absent in sleeping/sedated patients. Each F differs as different AHC population backfires; ≥10 stimuli; ≤0.5 Hz.
- p.44–45: F checks the entire nerve (prolonged with distal slowing/polyneuropathy/height). F estimate = (2D/CV)×10 + 1 ms + DL (1 ms AHC turnaround).
- p.46: F useful in early GBS (proximal demyelination; routine NCS normal with prolonged/absent F). Not obtainable when CMAP severely reduced. Insensitive for radiculopathy (Mauricio: tibial F prolonged in 4% of EMG-confirmed S1 radic; 8% with F-estimate); muscles have ≥2 myotomes. H reflex is a true reflex (Ia afferent, synapse, motor efferent); beyond age 2 routinely tibial→soleus only; 1 ms pulse selectively activates Ia.
- p.47–48 (Fig 4.8, 4.11): H at low intensity without M; increasing stim → M appears, H decreases through collision with antidromic motor volley; supramaximal: H disappears, replaced by F. H latency 25–34 ms; sweep 10 ms; gain 200–500 µV.
- p.49: side-to-side H difference >1.5 ms significant; H = electrical correlate of ankle reflex; bilaterally absent H in elderly not necessarily abnormal; H/M ratio ↑ in UMN lesions. Axon reflex: identical latency/configuration each trace; seen in reinnervated nerves with submaximal stim (p.49–50).

## Ch 6 RNS
- p.59 (Fig 6.2): EPP vs threshold model. Postsynaptic (MG): fewer AChRs → same quanta give lower EPP → reduced safety factor; slow RNS depletion → EPPs fall below threshold → MFAPs lost → decrement; secondary store mobilization after 5th–6th stimulus → U-shaped decrement. Presynaptic (LEMS): ↓Ca entry (VGCC antibodies) → p and quanta released ↓ → baseline EPPs subthreshold → low baseline CMAP; further decrement on slow RNS.
- p.60: Rapid RNS/brief exercise: mobilization + Ca accumulation ↑p → EPP ↑ → subthreshold EPPs reach threshold → increment. Maximal voluntary contraction firing 30–50 Hz. Postexercise facilitation (~10 s); postexercise exhaustion 2–4 min after 1 min exercise. Increments >100% not unusual in presynaptic disorders.
- p.61–62: Pseudofacilitation in normals (more synchronous MFAPs): amplitude ↑, duration ↓, area ~unchanged; ≤40%.
- p.62–63: Technical: immobilization; supramaximal stimuli (submaximal → artifactual decrement/increment); cold limb diminishes decrement — keep ≥33°C; withhold pyridostigmine 3–4 h. Proximal nerves higher yield in MG.
- p.64: Slow RNS 2–3 Hz; rapid 30–50 Hz; 5–10 pulses; lowest CMAP usually 4th; decrement % = (baseline − lowest)/baseline ×100; >10% abnormal; U-shape (mobilization 1–2 s).
- p.65: Increment % = (highest − baseline)/baseline ×100; up to 40% normal (pseudofacilitation), 40–100% equivocal, >100% often presynaptic. Decrement also in MND / denervation-reinnervation (immature NMJs), myotonic disorders, metabolic myopathies.

## Ch 7 Anomalous innervation
- p.67–68: Martin-Gruber anastomosis (15–30%) → ulnar pseudo-conduction block wrist→below-elbow; up to 10% ulnar drop wrist–BE normal.
- p.57: NMJ: quantum ≈10,000 ACh molecules; immediately available store ≈1000 quanta; mobilization store ≈10,000; reserve >100,000. EPP ∝ ACh bound; safety factor = EPP amplitude above threshold.
- p.58: Teaching model: m = p·n, p≈0.2, n≈1000 baseline; mobilization starts after 1–2 s; Ca clearance ≈100 ms (rate >10 Hz → Ca accumulation ↑p). Worked example: 200 quanta → EPP 40 mV, threshold 15 mV (illustrative in text); MG example same quanta → 20 mV.

## Ch 8 Technical factors
- p.78: Cooling delays Na-channel inactivation → slower nodal depolarization → slower CV; ~linear 21–34°C; CV slows 1.5–2.5 m/s per °C, DL +0.2 ms/°C; larger, longer fiber APs → higher amplitude & longer duration (SNAP > CMAP, less phase cancellation).
- p.79 (Box 8.2): cool → slow CV, prolonged DL, ↑amplitude & duration; MUAP duration/amplitude/phases ↑. Maintain 32–34°C. Pitfalls: false polyneuropathy, false CTS, axonal neuropathy slowed into demyelinating range.
- p.80–81: Age: neonatal CV ~half adult; adult >60 y: −0.5–4.0 m/s/decade; SNAP amplitude may fall up to 50% by age 70; MUAP duration ↑ with age. Height: taller → slower; legs slower than arms.
- p.83: Filters: motor 10 Hz–10 kHz; sensory 20 Hz–2 kHz. Lowering LFF → duration ↑ slightly (duration is lower-frequency); lowering HFF → amplitude ↓ (amplitude is higher-frequency). Use standardized filters.
- p.97 (Fig 8.33–8.34): ↑sensitivity (gain) → measured onset latency shortens; slower sweep → measured latency lengthens. Peak latency unaffected.

## Ch 12 EMG overview
- p.135–136 (Fig 12.3–12.4): concentric needle: G1 = central wire at bevelled tip, G2 = shaft; teardrop recording field; MUAP amplitude derived primarily from fibers near tip (90%/99% amplitude contours); monopolar larger field.
- p.136–137 (Box 12.1): rest/insertion: 10 ms/div, 50 µV/div; MUAPs: 10 ms/div, 200 µV/div. Normal insertional activity ≤300 ms after needle stops; spontaneous activity = activity at rest lasting >3 s. Closer needle → higher amplitude, shorter rise time ("sharp"). Normally firing frequency and number of MUAPs increase with force; ~10–20 (ideally 20) MUAPs sampled.

## Ch 14 Spontaneous activity
- p.231–232 (Table 14.1, Fig 14.4): Morphology identifies generator (MFAP brief spike/positive wave vs MUAP vs time-linked fibers), but stability and firing pattern needed. Endplate spike: biphasic initial negative (starts under needle); fibrillation: triphasic initial positive (starts at distance). Firing <4–5 Hz cannot be voluntary. Neuromyotonia 150–250 Hz.
- p.232: Normal MUAP 2–4 phases, 5–15 ms duration.
- p.233: Endplate noise = MEPPs, monophasic negative, 20–40 Hz irregular ("seashell"); endplate spikes irregular up to 50 Hz, initial negative, needle-induced terminal twig irritation. Any spontaneous activity outside endplate zone >3 s is abnormal. Increased insertional activity in neuropathic & myopathic disorders.
- p.234: Fibrillation = single muscle fiber; marker of active denervation; also inflammatory myopathies, dystrophies, rarely severe NMJ disease (botulism). Brief spike, initial positive, 1–5 ms, 10–100 µV; very regular 0.5–10 Hz (occ. 30 Hz); "rain on the roof"; very chronic (>6–12 mo) tiny <10 µV. PSW: brief positivity then long negative; 10–100 µV (occ. up to 3 mV); regular 0.5–10 Hz; may appear alone early.
- p.235–236 (Fig 14.13–14.14): PSW = needle-deformed ("crushed zone") membrane; same fiber recorded at distance = fibrillation. Grading 0–+4. Timing depends on nerve length between lesion and muscle: L5–S1 root lesion → paraspinals 10–14 days, proximal thigh 2–3 wk, leg 3–4 wk, distal leg/foot 5–6 wk; lesion near NMJ (botulism) → a few days.
- p.236–238 (Fig 14.16–14.18): CRD = single fiber depolarization with ephaptic spread to adjacent denervated fibers, circus movement re-activating pacemaker; 5–100 Hz; perfectly regular, abrupt onset/offset, machine-like; may change in quantal jumps; chronic neuropathic and myopathic; needs adjacent denervated fibers (fiber-type grouping then grouped atrophy) — not in acute setting; low jitter.
- p.238: Myotonic discharge = muscle fiber; waxing/waning amplitude & frequency 20–150 Hz; "revving engine"; myotonic dystrophy, myotonia congenita, paramyotonia; also some myopathies, HyperPP, rarely brief runs in denervation. Fasciculation = single spontaneous discharge of a motor unit, slow irregular (<1–2 Hz); generator motor neuron or axon (most distal axon); MUAP morphology; in MND but also radiculopathy, polyneuropathy, entrapment, and normal individuals (benign); "corn popping".
- p.239–242: doublets/triplets (tetany); myokymia 5–60 Hz bursts, <2 Hz between; cramp 40–75 Hz; neuromyotonic 150–250 Hz decrementing.

## Ch 15 MUAP analysis
- p.245: Fibers per motor unit: 5–10 (laryngeal) to ~2000 (soleus); adult territory 5–10 mm; fibers of 5–6 units intermix ("mosaic"); two fibers of same unit rarely adjacent. Variability in fiber depolarization times due to terminal axon length and NMJ transmission time.
- p.246: Size principle: small (low threshold, slow CV, type I) units recruited first. MUAP recorded outside membrane is 1/10–1/100 transmembrane amplitude and falls rapidly with distance. Quantitative: 20 MUAPs. Proximal muscles shorter MUAPs. Motor unit loss ~1%/yr from 3rd decade.
- p.247 (Table 15.1, Fig 15.4): MUAP duration age/muscle dependent; wide normal distribution — judge on mean of many MUAPs. Duration = initial deflection → final return to baseline; best reflects number of fibers in unit; typical 5–15 ms; depends on fiber number and temporal dispersion (endplate scatter, terminal lengths, CVs); ↑ with age, ↑ with cooling.
- p.248: Phases = baseline crossings + 1; normal 2–4; polyphasia up to 5–10% normal (deltoid 25%); polyphasia = measure of synchrony, nonspecific (myopathic & neuropathic). Satellite potentials = early reinnervation via slowly conducting immature sprouts; time-locked, unstable; mature into the main complex. Amplitude usually >100 µV and <2 mV.
- p.249: Amplitude reflects only 2–12 fibers nearest the needle (tissue = high-frequency filter); depends on proximity, fiber number, fiber diameter, synchrony. Major spike rise time <500 µs = needle close ("sharp").
- p.250–251 (Fig 15.10–15.11): Needle position changes amplitude markedly, duration relatively unaffected. Unstable MUAPs = unstable NMJs (primary NMJ disease, early reinnervation). Voluntary MUAPs semirhythmic.
- p.251–252 (Fig 15.13–15.14): Force ↑ by recruiting more units or firing faster (twitch summation; tetanic fusion ~30–50 Hz). Electrical MUAP 5–15 ms, mechanical twitch >100 ms. Onset firing 4–5 Hz.
- p.253: Firing rate : number of MUAPs ≈ 5:1 (2nd unit at ~10 Hz, 3rd at ~15 Hz); max 30–50 Hz (ballistic ~100 Hz; soleus ~15 Hz). Activation = central ability to increase firing rate (poor in CNS disease, pain, poor cooperation, functional). Recruitment = adding MUAPs as rate increases; reduced in neuropathic disease (axonal loss or conduction block), rarely end-stage myopathy. Fig 15.15: normal/neuropathic/myopathic/central interference patterns.
- p.254 (Fig 15.16): One MUAP at 30 Hz = reduced recruitment; one MUAP at 5 Hz = reduced activation; 18 Hz single unit = both (ALS, or pain + neuropathy). "Early recruitment" = too many MUAPs for the force generated (myopathy, some NMJ disease); recruitment relative to firing rate is actually appropriate; judged by examiner who knows force. Recruitment best judged at moderate contraction (single MUAP at 15–20 Hz = reduced).
- p.255: Acute axonal loss: motor Wallerian degeneration first 3–5 days; acute → normal MUAP morphology with reduced recruitment only; morphology changes take weeks–months. Same pattern with demyelinating conduction block.
- p.255–256: Complete denervation → regrowth from injury ≤1 mm/day; partial → collateral sprouting → long-duration, high-amplitude, polyphasic MUAPs + reduced recruitment (never acute; at least several weeks, usually months). Pure demyelination with slowing only → normal EMG; with block → reduced recruitment, normal morphology.
- p.256–257: Myopathy: fewer functioning fibers per unit → short, small, polyphasic MUAPs; number of units normal → recruitment normal for activation but early relative to force. Chronic myopathy (necrotic/inflammatory) may show long/large units too; recruitment "too good" for the MUAP changes (e.g., IBM). End-stage myopathy may reduce recruitment.
- p.257 (Fig 15.18): Nascent units (early regrowth after severe/complete denervation): short, small, polyphasic but markedly reduced recruitment. NMJ: mild → normal; intermittent block → unstable MUAPs; persistent block → short/small/polyphasic, normal/early recruitment; severe block (botulism) → reduced recruitment. CNS: normal morphology & recruitment, reduced activation.

## Ch 16 Clinical–electrophysiologic correlations
- p.260: Wallerian degeneration motor days 3–5, sensory 6–10; motor amplitudes decline earlier (NMJ failure first). Decreased recruitment in weak muscles immediately at lesion onset; spontaneous activity takes weeks (length-dependent).
- p.261 (Table 16.1): time-related axonal loss: NCS normal <3 days distal; spontaneous activity 10–14 d (paraspinal) to 5–6 wk (foot) for root lesion; reinnervation over months → long/large/polyphasic MUAPs; months–years later spontaneous activity disappears, reinnervated MUAPs + reduced recruitment remain; amplitudes may improve. Demyelination: slowing alone → no fixed weakness; conduction block → weakness/numbness; acquired vs inherited (uniform slowing).
- p.262 (Fig 16.2): demyelination distribution: diffuse vs distal only (prolonged DL, normal CV) vs proximal only (normal routine NCS, prolonged late responses). Pure demyelination with block: needle EMG only reduced recruitment; slowing alone → normal EMG. Most demyelinating lesions have secondary axonal loss.
- p.263–264: Patterns: hyperacute (<3 d) axonal; acute (days–weeks: NCS low amp, EMG reduced recruitment only; fibs need 2–6 wk depending on length); subacute (fibs, normal MUAPs); subacute–chronic (fibs + long/large MUAPs; most polyneuropathies); chronic (no fibs, reinnervated MUAPs persist indefinitely, amplitudes may recover).
- p.265: proximal demyelinating lesion with block: distal NCS normal; F/H abnormal; block across lesion; EMG reduced recruitment only; <4 days history → indistinguishable from hyperacute axonal loss; repeat at 1 week. Distal demyelinating lesion (CTS): DL ↑↑, distal amplitudes ↓, forearm motor CV normal (DL subtracted out).
- p.266: Distal slowing alone: motor amplitude normal, sensory amplitude often ↓ (dispersion/cancellation). Nascent units pattern. Myopathy: sensory always normal; motor usually normal (proximal involvement); EMG short/small/polyphasic, normal/early recruitment.
- p.267: Myopathies with inflammatory/necrotic features (polymyositis, toxic, dystrophies) → fibs/PSWs; others → myotonia. Chronic myopathy with denervating features → mixed MUAP populations, CRDs; IBM can mimic MND except recruitment.
- p.268: NMJ: sensory normal, DL/CV/late responses normal. Postsynaptic: CMAP normal, 3 Hz decrement >10%; presynaptic: CMAP ↓ at rest, decrement at 3 Hz, increment usually >100% after 50 Hz RNS or brief exercise. Fibs only in botulism. Unstable MUAPs. UMN lesions: normal NCS & morphology, decreased activation, erratic firing.

## Localization
- p.561: SNAPs normal in radiculopathy (lesion proximal to DRG), abnormal in axonal lesions at/distal to DRG — correct >99% of the time; rare exception superficial peroneal SNAP in L5 radiculopathy (intraforaminal DRG, p.561–562).
- p.563: Radiculopathy EMG: myotomal pattern beyond any one nerve; sample muscles of same myotome via different nerves (exclude mononeuropathy), proximal & distal (exclude length-dependent polyneuropathy), adjacent myotomes; paraspinals (dorsal rami) — only other finding besides normal SNAPs that conclusively separates radiculopathy from plexopathy; abnormal in only ~50% of radiculopathies; post-surgical paraspinals unreliable.
- p.566: Radiculopathy EMG: equivocal/false negatives not uncommon; single-level localization limited because most muscles are multi-myotomal.
- p.583–584 (Table 33.x): Brachial plexopathy: SNAPs abnormal (axonal lesions at/distal to DRG); SNAP pattern localizes plexus element; side-to-side comparison helpful.
- p.492, 495: Polyneuropathy: distal-to-proximal gradient; sural SNAP central; age effects on sural amplitude.
- p.528–534 (Ch 30): ALS: SNAPs normal (abnormal SNAPs should make one question ALS unless explained); EMG widespread active denervation + reinnervation in 3 of 4 segments (craniobulbar, cervical, thoracic, lumbosacral); revised Awaji: fasciculations count as active denervation with reinnervated MUAPs; thoracic paraspinal/bulbar never abnormal in isolated cervical/lumbar spine disease.
- p.503: MMNCB: motor conduction block, sensory sparing; autoimmune attack at nodes/paranodes ("nodopathies") — rapid IVIG response faster than remyelination would allow.
- p.499: AMAN = pure motor axonal GBS variant.

## Ch 37 NMJ / SFEMG
- p.658: Jitter = variation in interval between firing of adjacent fibers of the same motor unit, primarily variation in NMJ transmission time; blocking = fiber fails to reach AP. Increased jitter has no clinical correlate; blocking correlates with weakness; SFEMG abnormal before RNS. Unstable MUAPs.
- p.659: SFEMG: LFF raised to 500–1000 Hz; records fibers within 200–300 µm; MCD over 50–100 discharges; 20 pairs; normal limits age/muscle dependent (Table 37.3).
- p.660: abnormal if mean MCD abnormal or >10% pairs exceed limit; blocking usually when MCD >80–100 µs.
- p.662: SFEMG cannot distinguish MG vs LEMS.
- p.681: Increased jitter/blocking also in myopathy — sensitive but not specific to NMJ disease. (Reinnervation: unstable immature NMJs, p.250.)

## Ch 38 Myopathy
- p.677: Sensory NCS always normal (unless coexistent neuropathy); motor usually normal.
- p.678: Normal or near-normal EMG most commonly in steroid myopathy and some metabolic/mitochondrial myopathies.
- p.680: In myopathy some MUAPs may be normal or even long while mean duration decreases.
- p.683: Abundant spontaneous activity favours polymyositis over steroid myopathy.
